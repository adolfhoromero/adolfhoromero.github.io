A little conquest in Lansing

Cargill, Inc., a multinational empire,
seeks free rein in the local salt mine

to gain complete power to determine who works in
what job and for how long, for how much pay and for
what benefits, and with what hazards to life and
health. In short, they want to get free rein in the
workplace by consolidating their control over the

lives of the workers.

What Cargill is really “offering”
The terrns of management's offer indicate Car-

gill's aims clearly. Wages are not the issue: Cargill
has already agreed to a wage increase. The major
sticking points are four Cargill proposals that, taken
as a package, would render the workers powerless
even if the union were somehow to survive,

It is important to look at the proposals in detail,
since newspaper accounts that describe the dispute as
“involving issues of seniority” can make it sound as
though Cargill is simply trying to rescue the career
opportunities of newer workers from the entrenched
privileges of older workers. As such, busting the
union might seem like a favor to the workforce, since
Cargill would be replacing a policy of “protecting the
dead wood” with a system of rewards by merit. In
this way, superficial reporting can serve as
propaganda for the cause of the Cargill empire.

One part of Cargill's package involves the way
new job openings are to be filled. At first glance, the
proposal seems like it would fulfill the American
dream of advancement according to merit. Bids for
jobs would be judged according to the skills that the
bidders possess. If no single bidder excelled the
others by this criterion, Cargill would use the
criterion of “overall ability to perform the job.”
Finally, if these criteria produced no winner, Cargill
would look for the most senior bidder among those
bidders who are currently employed in the mine.

in fixed assets like buildings and machinery: Cargill
deals with cash, and lots of it. As a result, the
company finds it easy to move its assets around, and
this means that Cargill can afford to pour money into
operations that are losing money temporarily—as the
Lansing mine may be doing right now. Thus,
Cargill, as a trading company, resembles a big bank
as much as it resembles a big manufacturing
company: to lay out a large amount of cash (to buy a
million tons of wheat, or to survive a strike) in order
to reap a huge profit later (by selling the wheat abroad
when prices go up, or by busting the mineworkers'
union) is Cargill's standard business practice, and the
company is specially set up to operate this way.

Cargill's other money advantage comes from
being privately owned. As such, the MacMillan and
Cargill families do not have to attract stockholders by
paying large dividends. In fact, families controlling
such empires tend to shelter themselves from taxes by
undervaluing their assets and paying themselves low
dividends. The extra money is reinvested in the
company, and in the case of Cargill, the capital is
reserved as cash for contingencies like a sweet
purchase of soybeans—or for weathering a strike.

There is another advantage to being privately
owned: secrecy. Cargill is not required to publish
detailed figures on any of its operations. In fact,
when the mineworkers' negotiating committee asked
to inspect the books that Cargill keeps for the mine
(a standard practice to insure that a company is really
in the shape it claims to be in), Cargill refused. The
Cargills and MacMillans lead very private lives, after
all, and the world's largest grain traders are not
accountable to anyone.

Aside from its riches and its secrecy, Cargill
holds a third advantage over the mineworkers. It
looks as though the strikers’ jobs may depend on a
case pending with the National Labor Relations Board
(NLRB), and all five of NLRB’s national board mem-
bers are Reagan appointees. The point of law in
question is whether the strike is motivated by the mi-
ners’ economic demands or whether it is a response to
Unfair Labor Practices (ULP’s). If the strike is de-
clared economic, Cargill is not obliged to re-hire any
strikers, but if it is judged a ULP strike, Cargill must

take everyone back. (When Cargill offered to take
back the 47 miners, it also demanded that the ULP
cases be dropped. Cargill was asking a minority of
the strikers to sell out the jobs of the majority.)

The union has already won a ULP case charging
Cargill with bad-faith bargaining; Cargill cancelled
out on six negotiating sessions and then sprang its
“offer” on the union just a few days before the last
contract expired. The regional NLRB told Cargill to
behave better in the future. The Reagan-appointed
national board is to decide whether this ULP, plus
another charge of bad-faith bargaining (Cargill
negotiators held up the start of a bargaining session
until 2 a.m.), substantiate the union's claim that this
is a “ULP strike.” A victory at the NLRB would not
settle the strike in the union's favor, but it would
assure the mineworkers that they won't lose their jobs
permanently while bargaining coritinues.

Why others should join the fight:
the imperatives of solidarity

Members of the community, of course, have a
morai obigaiion to help out their neighbors in ime

f need, but there are imperatives of seli-intercsi, too.
The dispute is not an isolated disagreement between 2
company and a few workers; its outcome will have
effects on similar struggles elsewhere. Labor defeats
in a few local industries can depress wages and affect
employment patterns throughout the area. We stress
again that the miners’ struggle is a political struggle
for power over their lives, and it is part of a broader
local and national struggle in which a wealthy
minority has the upper hand against the majority.
The front line of this struggle is often the picket line,
and we know from experience that broad-based
support for those at the front line can turn the tide.
In this area, the front line is now in Lansing.
