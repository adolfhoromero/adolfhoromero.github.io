Re: Smith-Corona Layoffs

Dear Coert:

I have been reading WARN ("Worker Adjustment and
Retraining Notification Act") regulations and legislative
history since our meeting on the 28th. As a result, I have
gotten a very different (and more optimistic) sense of what we
may be able to do with the statute. I am writing this letter
to highlight a few of the more interesting things I have
learned and to let you know how I see us as fitting into your
endeavors at this stage of things.

You may remember that an employer has to give notice in
the event of either a "plant closing" or "mass layoff"
involving 50 or more employees. A plant closing is basically
defined as a shutdown of a "Single site of employment" ("SSE")
or of a "facility" or “operating unit" within an SSE. A mass
layoff means a reduction in force at an SSE of either 33% of
the employees or 500 employees. Note that only the SSE (not
the facility or operating unit) matters in the mass layoff
case. That is, you have to lose 33% of the workers at the
whole site to have a mass layoff; 33% of a particular facility
or operating unit won't get you anywhere. For this reason,
you can actually have a plant closing that is too small to be
amass layoff.

What makes things interesting is the way the regula-
tions define SSE, facility, and operating unit.

Lets take SSE first. This is a fairly complicated term
but an SSE is basically a geographically unified physical

For example, suppose there are four operating units at
an SSE, each employing 400 workers. If one of the operating
units is shut down, you have a plant closing. You do not have
a mass layoff, however, because you have lost neither 1/3 of
the work force nor 500 workers.

employing different workers, are separate SSEs. Thus, there
would be no mass layoff if Smith-Corona laid off 350 of 1600
workers at a single industrial park. But change the facts
slightly so that the company lays off 300 of 800 workers at
its "East Side" plant and 50 of 800 workers at its "West Side"
plant and you have a mass layoff at the East Side location.
Therefore, the physical distribution of Smith-Corona plants
and warehouses in and around the City of Cortland becomes
quite important. If they're all together in one spot, we
probably don't have much. If they're spread out a bit,
however, we may have a mass layoff.

The next term is "facility." It basically means
"building." (Again, the term matters for plant closing
purposes but not for mass layoff purposes.) Thus, if the
employer shuts down operations in a whole building, laying off
50 or more workers in the process, you've got yourself a plant
closing.

The final term and the one most difficult to define is
“operating unit." (Again, operating units only come into play
in plant closing situations.) The official definition
contained in the regulations is "an organizationally or
operationally distinct product, operation, or specific work
function within or across facilities at the single site." A
few specific examples are provided to elucidate this defini-
tion: A company's data processing department is an operating
unit but not the smaller sub-units of data entry workers,
computer programmers, etc. An assembly line in an automobile
manufacturing plant is an operating unit. The smaller group
of workers which puts on the doors or bumpers is not. A
particular shift is not ordinarily an operating unit unless it
performs a different operation from the other shifts. Thus,
the third shift in an assembly line is not an operating unit
but the maintenance shift is. Similarly, if a company decides
to contract out security services and lays off the security
force, it has shut down an operating unit. There is obviously
a lot of ambiguity in this concept and things get fuzzy when
it comes to drawing the line between an assembly line, which
usually is an operating unit, and a shift, which normally
isn't, especially in situations where different shifts may
work on different products. In sum, whether a plant closing
has taken place will depend on exactly who was laid off and
exactly what they were doing.

gative work that must be done in order to determine whether or
not we have a lawsuit. We need to know (1) the physical
location of every building in which the manufacturing of
product took place, (2) the distribution of workers among the

I don't think that we can bring a lawsuit until after
we have obtained fairly strong evidence of a violation. [In
other words, I don't think we can bring a lawsuit blind, for
the purpose of using the suit to gain access to company
records in order to discover if WARN violations have taken
place. There are a number of reasons why I feel this way,
which I would be happy to discuss with you in more detail, but
I will mention at least one which I learned about in the
course of my research: In a WARN lawsuit, the judge may award
attorney's fees, not just to a prevailing plaintiff, but to
the prevailing party, i.e., to whichever side wins. This
means that if we bring an "exploratory" suit and there turns
out to be nothing there, there is potential liability on the
part of TCLC, if named as a party, and/or the individual named
plaintiffs, which could amount to many tens of thousands of
dollars. On the other hand, we could use a small WARN
violation of whose existence we were reasonably certain to
bring the lawsuit and then explore for additional violations
involving other workers. For example, if we knew that Smith-
Corona had completely shutdown a "facility" housing 75
workers, we could use that as the basis for launching the

litigation, hoping to discover evidence of further violations
as the suit progressed.

Mimi and I have discussed our possible role in this
business and our feelings are essentially as follows: We
don't want to play an active role in investigating whether,
factually speaking, WARN violations have occurred. This would 4
be extremely time-consuming; and work of this sort, though s
challenging and intellectually demanding, doesn't particularly
call upon the kind of "“lawyerly" skills we possess. What we
would like to see happen is for someone else to organize the
investigation and gather the data, bringing it to us at the
end to analyze from a legal point of view and determine if it

This is an unusual provision, born of the compromises
agreed to to get the WARN legislation through Congress. In
most statutes where the plaintiff can get attorney's fees, the
defendant can't get them unless the judge decides the lawsuit
was utterly baseless. Here, however, the Plaintiff and
defendant are on exactly equal footing. While I am inclined

to think most judges would hesitate to impose fees on workers
who brought suit in good faith and lost, the exposure is
certainly there.
