1990 Negotiations Notebook, Tompkins-Seneca-Tioga BOCES Employees' Association

February 21, 1990

A training notebook the Coalition and Cornell's ILR School prepared for the BOCES Employees' Association before its 1990 contract talks. These pages hold the cover, the agenda for an evening negotiations training on February 21, 1990 (preparing for bargaining, the legal side, the mechanics of bargaining, and a mock negotiation), a model request to the employer for wage, benefit, and personnel information, and typed notes on bargaining under New York's Taylor Law: the duty to bargain in good faith, mandatory and non-mandatory subjects, impasse procedures for school districts, strike penalties, and improper practices.

The notebook also reproduces pages of a union's published bargaining manual; those pages are withheld for copyright. A second photograph of the cover is not shown.

Bargainin nder the Taylor Law
Duty to bargain

The employer must bargain with the union "in good faith” over
"terms and conditions" of employment. The union has the
exclusive right to bargain for all employees in the negotiating
unit.

"Good faith" means that the employer must "approach the
bargaining table with a sincere desire to reach an agreement”.
Refusing to bargain in good faith is an "improper practice” under
the Taylor Law, §209-a.2(b). The following examples can serve as
evidence of bad faith intentions:

- Refusing to supply information requested by the union and
necessary for bargaining.

- Refusing to meet at reasonable times, or attempting to dictate
who or how many may be on the union team.

—~ Refusing to abide by ground rules.

- Attempting to bargain directly with the membership instead of
bargaining with the negotiating team.

- Attempting to discourage membership support for the union
through threats, promises, punishment, or discrimination.

- Withdrawing approval of particular parts of the contract on
which the two sides had already reached tentative agreement.

Refusing to negotiate over a mandatory subject of bargaining.
Making unilateral changes in mandatory bargaining subjects

before bargaining to impasse or without talking to the
union (unless the union has waived the merent) .

- Refusing to negotiate over the effects of unilateral changes in
non-mandatory subjects.

Subcontracting is one of the most ambiguous subjects. If it is
done soley for economic reasons, it is usually mandatory. But if
non-unit employees do the same work, and the employer wants to
use fewer unit employees and more non-unit, it is not mandatory.
Also, if the subcontracting is due to a change of services or
results in significant changes in the job qualifications, it may
be considered non-mandatory.
