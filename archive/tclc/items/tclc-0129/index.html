since the Great Depression. More than 2.3 million manufacturing jobs %
were lost. It was also the decade that witnessed the emergence of

The 1980s were also incredibly wasteful. Many companies closed
because of mismanagement or narrow corporate strategies that placed
the highest return in the next quarter over the needs of the company,
the workers, or the community. We saw enormous incentive packages
thrown at employers with no strings attached and no cost-benefit
analyses done to safeguard taxpayers’ investments. We saw governors
and mayors held hostage by employers seeking to expand or threatening
to relocate, and a "hands-off" attitude from Washington which only
fueled the Economic Development Civil War.

Whatever progress that was made on the federal level to help
dislocated workers was the culmination of twenty years of state and
local experimentation, in response to growing public discontent. The
best ideas "trickled up," and that is a hopeful sign that further
improvements will continue under the new Worker Adjustment and
Retraining Notification (WARN) Act and the new Economic Dislocation
Worker Adjustment Assistance (EDWAA) Act. States continue to lead
the way in dislocated worker policy innovations, and we outline here
some of the emerging issues.

The 1990s are the time to build on these early hesitant steps
with policies to improve the performance of economic development
programs and programs to prevent unnecessary plant closings and the
dislocation of workers. We outline several promising methods already
in place at the state level which are gaining wider attention. These
can be the building blocks of an action agenda on a local level that

can lead to a comprehensive and effective national policy.

. Finally, although we have tried to uncover every law relevant to
this subject, there has been a great deal of activity in this field
recently, and we may have missed something. If you have news of
other developments in plant Closings laws or development subsidy

restrictions, we'd like to hear from you.
Be. cL -

Dan Swinney
Acting Director

The F ederation for Industrial Retention and Renewal, or FIRR, was founded in 1988
to provide national organizing Support and policy development for local grassroots
organizations fighting plant closings and other effects of the U.S. industrial crisis.

After years of waging local battles, the 15 local groups which founded FIRR felt the
time was right to join together to share organizing experiences and strategies and
build a national industrial agenda to save jobs and communities.
