About My Journey

Biography

I am a PhD student in Cornell University’s School of Industrial and Labor Relations. I study how worker centers and community organizations help immigrant, low-wage, and undocumented workers build voice, leadership, and collective power—especially when traditional union protections are out of reach. Drawing on oral history, ethnography, and the Power Resource Approach, I look closely at how grassroots organizations create spaces of dignity and democratic participation within unequal labor systems.

This work is personal to me. I come from a working-class Mexican and Salvadoran family in Humboldt Park, Chicago, where I saw how hard people worked and how often their knowledge went unrecognized. Years of fieldwork—from documenting Black resistance in the Mississippi Delta to collaborating with Indigenous communities through the Doris Duke Oral History Project—have deepened that commitment. I approach labor scholarship as a form of listening: grounded in collective memory, accountable to the people who share their stories, and connected to struggles for dignity and justice.

Ethical commitment: I follow IRB-approved protocols and oral-consent practices, but ethics goes beyond compliance. It means treating participants as partners, respecting their dignity, and remaining responsible for how their stories are represented.

Conference · October 14–17, 2026

Oral History Association Annual Meeting

Presenter · Roundtable participantHilton Portland Downtown · Portland, Oregon

I presented twice at the Oral History Association’s 60th Annual Meeting, “Landscapes of Memory,” in Portland. The first was a roundtable on the Underground Railroad Oral History Project with Deborah Hendrix, Ronan Hart, and colleagues from UF’s Samuel Proctor Oral History Program and Center for African Studies. I spent years at SPOHP recording interviews with descendants of freedom seekers, and sitting at the table with that group again, seeing how much the project has grown, felt a little like coming home.

My second presentation, “From Crisis to Care: Community Interviews on Worker Centers and Collective Power,” followed worker centers and collective power in Tompkins County from the 1980s to today. It brought my thesis research into conversation with oral history, asking what workers’ own accounts of care, struggle, and solidarity add to how we understand organizing. Between sessions I caught up with colleagues from UF, Virginia Tech, and Oklahoma State.

OHA meets in Chicago, my hometown, in 2027. I’d like to bring a panel on labor, oral history, and organizing. If you’d like to be part of that conversation, send me a note.

Conference · September 18, 2026

Migration in Contentious Political Times

Co-organizer & panel moderatorAtkinson Hall · Cornell University

With support from the Migrations Program at Cornell’s Einaudi Center for International Studies, Vicente Celestino Mata, Jr. and I organized a full-day conference that brought together scholars, students, practitioners, and community members. We talked through migration, labor, displacement, borders, belonging, law, inequality, and the political forces shaping the lives of migrants and workers today.

People came from history, anthropology, law, public policy, global development, literature, economics, and labor relations. I’m grateful to everyone who presented, moderated, brought posters, and stayed for the conversation, and especially to Vicente, Katie Fiorella, and Kathy Peck, whose coordination made the day possible.

Keynote

“Seeing the Americas Whole”: Richard Rodriguez’s Thesis on Migrants and Migration in the Era of American Carnage

Paul Ortiz, Professor of Labor History, Cornell ILR. Drawing on testimonios and novelists including Carlos Fuentes, Edwige Danticat, and Julissa Arce, the keynote took up assimilation, today’s anti-immigrant politics, and the future of the labor movement.

October 202651(2)Aztlán: A Journal of Chicano Studies · UCLA
PublishedCo-author with Shannon Gleeson

Latinx Labor Rights and Workplace Enforcement

Workers in the US have the right to the minimum wage, overtime, and a safe workplace, but those rights only mean something if someone enforces them. Latinx workers are among the most likely to see them go unenforced. Many work in agriculture, construction, meatpacking, and domestic work, where wage theft and injuries are common, and many face language barriers, fear of retaliation, or immigration threats when they speak up.

In our article for the journal’s Latinx labor studies dossier, Shannon Gleeson and I look at how enforcement works on the ground, where it breaks down, and what workers, worker centers, and advocates have built to fill the gaps.

Thanks to Shannon, to Chris Zepeda-Millán for editing the dossier, and to Paul Ortiz for his feedback on the piece.

August–September 2026Research team · Ithaca Co-Lab

Tompkins County Minimum Wage Study

Following Resolution 2024-144, Tompkins County asked the Cornell ILR Ithaca Co-Lab to explore a countywide minimum wage above New York’s $16.00. Between March and June our team held eight public convenings and six focus groups with businesses, farms, care providers, public-benefits staff, young workers, and low-wage workers, and gathered more than 100 survey responses and 11 recorded testimonies. We shared the findings at a final public convening at the Tompkins County Public Library on August 27.

I co-authored the final report with Ian Greer, David Bateman, Wen Li Thian, and Matt Vidal, writing the sections on what workers told us. It was released on September 4 and taken up by the County Legislature in September.

Inside the thesis archive

Reading worker power through its own press.

My thesis research follows how workers and community organizations in Tompkins County built collective capacity beyond the traditional union hall. The Working Press and The Workers’ Edge show how those groups explained workplace struggles, connected campaigns, and created a public language of solidarity.

The question guiding this work: What kinds of power become possible when workers build their own institutions, media, relationships, and memory?
